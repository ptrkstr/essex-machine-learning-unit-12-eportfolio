Correlation and Regression¶

Unit 3 e-Portfolio activity for Machine Learning (PCOM7E). The activity supplies four programmes and asks that they be run and their variables changed, so that the effect of altering the data points on correlation and regression can be observed. Each section below reproduces the supplied programme first, then varies its inputs and records what changed.

The four programmes are covariance and Pearson correlation, simple linear regression, multiple linear regression, and polynomial regression.

Dependencies¶

Requires numpy, pandas, scipy, scikit-learn and matplotlib. Install them into the environment before running, for example with pip install numpy pandas scipy scikit-learn matplotlib.

Varying the noise added to the second variable¶

The relationship between the two variables is fixed, so the only thing changing is how much independent noise is layered on top. Covariance and correlation are recomputed across a range of noise levels to separate what each statistic actually measures.

In [3]:
noiseLevels = [1, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100]
noiseResults = []

for noiseScale in noiseLevels:
    seed(1)
    firstSeries = 20 * randn(1000) + 100
    secondSeries = firstSeries + (noiseScale * randn(1000) + 50)
    covValue = nplib.cov(firstSeries, secondSeries)[0][1]
    corrValue, _ = pearsonr(firstSeries, secondSeries)
    noiseResults.append({"noise sd": noiseScale,
                         "covariance": round(covValue, 2),
                         "pearson r": round(corrValue, 4)})

noiseFrame = pdlib.DataFrame(noiseResults)
print("Effect of increasing independent noise")
print(sectionRule)
print(noiseFrame.to_string(index=False))

Varying the spread of the first variable¶

Here the noise is held at the supplied level and the standard deviation of the first variable is changed instead, which rescales the data without altering the strength of the underlying relationship.

In [4]:
spreadLevels = [5, 10, 20, 40, 80]
spreadResults = []

for spreadScale in spreadLevels:
    seed(1)
    firstSeries = spreadScale * randn(1000) + 100
    secondSeries = firstSeries + (10 * randn(1000) + 50)
    covValue = nplib.cov(firstSeries, secondSeries)[0][1]
    corrValue, _ = pearsonr(firstSeries, secondSeries)
    spreadResults.append({"sd of first series": spreadScale,
                          "covariance": round(covValue, 2),
                          "pearson r": round(corrValue, 4)})

spreadFrame = pdlib.DataFrame(spreadResults)
print("Effect of rescaling the first variable")
print(sectionRule)
print(spreadFrame.to_string(index=False))

Effect of rescaling the first variable
**********************************************************
 sd of first series  covariance  pearson r
                  5       25.19     0.4459
                 10       98.54     0.6979
                 20      389.75     0.8876
                 40     1550.18     0.9676
                 80     6183.01     0.9915

Observation. The two experiments separate the roles of the two statistics. Adding noise weakens the relationship and the correlation coefficient falls accordingly, while covariance rises only about eleven per cent across the same sweep in which the correlation coefficient falls by seventy nine per cent, because covariance is dominated by the shared variance the two series retain. Rescaling the first variable does the opposite: covariance moves substantially because it carries the units of both variables, whereas correlation barely moves because dividing by the two standard deviations cancels the scaling out. Covariance therefore reports the direction of a relationship but cannot be compared across differently scaled datasets, which is precisely why the normalised coefficient is the one reported in practice.

The supplied programme fits a straight line through thirteen observations of car age against speed, reports the correlation coefficient, and predicts the speed of a car aged ten years.

    axis.set_xlabel("age of car")

Observation. A single added observation, one fourteenth of the data, reverses the sign of the slope and turns a moderate negative relationship into a positive one. The prediction at age ten changes by a wide margin as a direct consequence. This is the clearest demonstration in the activity of why outlier screening belongs before model fitting rather than after: the fitted line is a summary of every point it is given, and least squares weights distant points heavily because it minimises squared error.

Observation. The coefficient attached to weight changes once volume joins the model, because the two predictors are correlated and each absorbs part of the other's effect when fitted alone. A coefficient is therefore a statement about a variable within a particular model rather than a fixed property of that variable, which matters whenever such a number is quoted as though it stood on its own.

The prediction sweep exposes a second issue. The supplied example asks for a vehicle weighing 3300 kg, while the heaviest car in the training data is considerably lighter. The model returns a value regardless, with nothing in the output signalling that the request sits outside the range the coefficients were estimated from.

fig.tight_layout()

Observation. The coefficient of determination rises monotonically with degree, because every additional term gives the curve another way to bend toward the observations. It is therefore a measure of how closely the curve reproduces the data it was fitted on, and cannot by itself indicate whether the curve would describe new observations. The plots show a high degree curve oscillating between points where the underlying relationship is plainly smooth, and the prediction at hour twenty five, just beyond the observed range, spans minus one hundred and thirty seven thousand to plus one thousand four hundred across the degrees tested, while the prediction at hour seventeen, inside the range, stays within a spread of under eight units. Extrapolation beyond the fitted range is where an overly flexible model fails most visibly.

What the four programmes show together¶

Running the four programmes with varied inputs produces a consistent theme. Each statistic answers a narrower question than its single reported number suggests. Covariance describes direction but carries the units of its inputs, so it cannot be compared across datasets. A correlation coefficient normalises that away but says nothing about the shape of a relationship. A regression coefficient is conditional on the other predictors in the model. The coefficient of determination measures fit to the training observations only, and rises automatically with model flexibility.

The linear regression experiment is the sharpest of the four: one point in fourteen reversed the direction of the fitted relationship. Taken together the four exercises argue that a model summary should be read alongside the data that produced it, particularly its range and its outliers, rather than in isolation.
