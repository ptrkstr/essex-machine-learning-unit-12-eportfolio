Linear Regression with Scikit-Learn¶

Unit 4 seminar activity for Machine Learning (PCOM7E). The activity asks for two tasks using the World Bank population and GDP files: investigate the correlation between the mean population of each country and its mean per capita GDP over 2001 to 2021, then fit a linear regression with mean population as the independent variable and mean per capita GDP as the dependent variable.

The supplied fuel consumption demonstration was reviewed separately and is kept unmodified alongside this notebook.

Dependencies¶

Requires numpy, pandas, scipy, scikit-learn and matplotlib.

Issue 1: the requested window is not fully covered. The activity asks for 2001 to 2021, but GDP coverage stops at 2020. The population mean is therefore computed over the full twenty one years and the GDP mean over the twenty years available, with the shortfall recorded rather than passed over.

The population file also ends with two provenance lines carrying no series name, and repeats one country. Both are removed below, keying on the missing series name rather than a missing country name, because the trailing lines do carry text in the country column.

Aggregate rows such as World, European Union and High income are not countries. Leaving them in would place a handful of enormous composite observations into a country level analysis and distort every statistic that follows.

Detection keys on the country name rather than a fixed list of codes. An earlier version of this notebook used a hand written code list and silently missed several aggregates, including Latin America and the Caribbean and the Pacific island small states, which then appeared among the largest observations in the analysis table. Matching on the naming patterns the World Bank uses for composite rows catches all of them while retaining countries whose names contain similar words, such as the Central African Republic and South Africa.

def toNumeric(frame, yearColumns):
    converted = frame.copy()
    for columnName in yearColumns:
        converted[columnName] = pdlib.to_numeric(converted[columnName], errors="coerce")
    return converted

populationBefore = populationCountries[populationYears].isna().sum().sum()

Deriving per capita GDP. The supplied GDP file reports total GDP in current US dollars rather than per capita, so the per capita figure has to be derived. Dividing the mean of total GDP by the mean of population is not the same as averaging the annual per capita values, so the per capita ratio is formed year by year first and averaged afterwards.

In [7]:
gdpAligned = gdpCountries.set_index("Country Code")
populationAligned = populationCountries.set_index("Country Code")
sharedCodes = gdpAligned.index.intersection(populationAligned.index)

perCapitaByYear = pdlib.DataFrame(index=sharedCodes)
for yearName in gdpYears:
    perCapitaByYear[yearName] = (
        gdpAligned.loc[sharedCodes, yearName] / populationAligned.loc[sharedCodes, yearName]
    )

perCapitaMeans = perCapitaByYear.mean(axis=1, skipna=True)

analysisTable = mergedTable.set_index("countryCode")
analysisTable["meanPerCapitaGdp"] = perCapitaMeans
analysisTable = analysisTable.dropna(subset=["meanPopulation", "meanPerCapitaGdp"])

print("Analysis table")
print(sectionRule)
print(f"countries with both measures: {len(analysisTable)}")
print()

The fit is extremely weak. The slope is a very small negative number and the coefficient of determination is close to zero, meaning mean population explains almost none of the variation in per capita GDP. Fitting the same regression on log transformed variables tests whether the weakness is caused by the skew rather than by an absence of relationship.

Results to bring to the seminar¶

Across 212 countries the Pearson coefficient on untransformed values is -0.0681 with a p value of 0.32, so on the measure the activity asks for there is no detectable linear association between population size and per capita GDP. The regression agrees: a coefficient of determination of 0.0046 means population explains under half of one per cent of the variation in national income per head.

The log transformed and rank based measures tell a more careful story and are worth raising in discussion. Both are around -0.29 to -0.31 and both are statistically significant, which indicates a weak negative monotonic tendency for larger countries to be poorer per head that the untransformed Pearson coefficient cannot detect, because the raw population values span six orders of magnitude and a straight line through them is dominated by two observations. The honest summary is therefore not that the variables are unrelated, but that any relationship is weak, non linear, and invisible to the specification the task prescribes.

Two points are worth raising for discussion. The first is that per capita GDP had to be derived, because the supplied file reports total GDP; forming the ratio year by year and then averaging is not equivalent to dividing one mean by the other, and the choice changes the resulting numbers. The second is that the requested 2001 to 2021 window cannot be honoured on the GDP side, which stops at 2020, so the two means are computed over slightly different periods. Both decisions are recorded above rather than absorbed silently, since either would change another team member's figures.
