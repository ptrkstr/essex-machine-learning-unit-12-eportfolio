# Collaborative Discussion 2: Legal and Ethical Views on ANN Applications

*Units 8 to 10.* The discussion weighed what AI writing tools offer against what they endanger, starting from Hutson (2021). I argued that the risk grows as tasks demand more judgement. The responses persuaded me that relying on each reader to stay sceptical is the weak point, and that safeguards belong in the design of the system.

## Initial Post (Unit 8)

[View the post on the module forum](https://www.my-course.co.uk/mod/forum/discuss.php?d=382436#p775478)

Hutson (2021) describes GPT-3 as a system that predicts text by observing statistical relationships between the words it has read, without understanding their meaning, and that distinction is what makes the risks and benefits of AI writers vary so sharply depending on the work being asked of them.

At the administrative end, the case is strong. Hutson (2021) reports developers testing GPT-3 on summarising legal documents and suggesting answers to customer service enquiries, and Noy and Zhang (2023) provide the experimental evidence in their study of college-educated professionals given occupation-specific writing tasks, where average time fell by 40 per cent and assessed quality rose by 18 per cent. The finding I consider more revealing sits underneath those headline numbers. Noy and Zhang (2023) tested whether participants treated the tool as an imperfect assistant, drafting with it and then improving the result, and found instead that almost everyone submitted its output either unedited or barely touched, with the editing that did occur being superficial and producing no measurable gain in grades. The productivity came from substitution rather than from collaboration, which is a less comfortable result than the time saving alone suggests.

The risks scale with how much interpretation the task demands. Bender et al. (2021) argue that such a model assembles word sequences purely from probabilities learned in training, with nothing underneath standing for what those words denote, and they name the result a stochastic parrot. The point they draw from this is that the coherence belongs to whoever is reading rather than to the system producing the text, so fluency carries no reliability with it. Hutson (2021) records the sharpest illustration, where a healthcare company prompted a GPT-3 chatbot with a question about suicide and received agreement. Fluent prose that is confidently wrong is more dangerous than obviously broken prose, because it defeats the reader's ordinary scepticism.

Creative writing occupies a more interesting middle position. Hutson (2021) cites a poet reporting that the model produced material worth editing more than half the time, which frames the tool as a generator of raw material rather than an author. My own view is that this is the honest framing for every level of use, and the difficulty is that the evidence above suggests it is not the framing people actually adopt. Where output is accepted as finished rather than worked on, the absence of meaning stops being a curiosity and becomes a liability, and that risk grows precisely as the task moves from administrative routine towards work where judgement is the point.

## Peer Responses (Unit 9)

### Response to Julio

[View the post on the module forum](https://www.my-course.co.uk/mod/forum/discuss.php?d=380409#p781972)

*In reply to Julio's post on language models as stochastic parrots.*

Your sparring partner framing describes how these tools ought to be used, and it is defensible on the technical evidence you cite. What complicates it is whether people work that way once the tool is in front of them.

Noy and Zhang (2023) put that to an experiment. They asked whether participants treated the assistant as an imperfect collaborator or simply passed its output onward, and the evidence supported the second reading: roughly a third submitted the output untouched, and those reporting edits were typically active for a few minutes on changes the authors call superficial, with no improvement in grades. Sparring needs an opponent who pushes back, and these participants did not push.

There is an established explanation for that. Parasuraman and Manzey (2010) review decades of evidence that operators lean on an automated system's answer instead of gathering and weighing information themselves, and that this complacency deepens when attention is divided across competing tasks rather than easing with practice. Read alongside Bender et al. (2021), whose point is that coherence is supplied by the reader rather than the system, the fluency of the output is precisely what suppresses the scrutiny it most requires.

This suggests measures aimed at the workflow rather than the model. Your deduplication and open architecture proposals address what a model can leak; provenance requirements and review steps where the reviewer answers for the claim rather than the prose address what the reader fails to do with it.

Disclosure is harder than it looks, though. Cheong et al. (2025) found that human and automated raters alike marked writing down once AI assistance was declared, so transparency carries a cost the honest author bears. Given your argument that creativity depends on cultural validation, does that penalty seem defensible to you, or does it simply discourage disclosure?

### Response to Lana

[View the post on the module forum](https://www.my-course.co.uk/mod/forum/discuss.php?d=389652#p797153)

*In reply to Lana's post on how far the safeguards in Hutson (2021) go.*

Your closing point, that current safeguards remain reactive rather than preventive, is the sharpest line in the post, and it sent me back to your own sources to see what a preventive version would actually look like.

McGuffie and Newhouse (2020) turn out to answer that directly. Their risk mitigation section sets out measures sitting at different points in the pipeline: consistent safeguards imposed by the creators and distributors of the models, detection and filtration systems deployed by service providers to reduce distribution, and critical digital literacy promoted through education and outreach. Reading them together supports your argument nicely, because only the first is available to any single deploying organisation, which is perhaps why the current picture looks as reactive as you describe.

Carlini et al. (2021) extend the same thought to the leakage risk you raise. They consider differential privacy, noting it prevents models from capturing the long tails of the data and so costs utility, and that applying it to web-scraped text is unclear in principle since a person's contact details may appear across many pages. Their more practical route is curation: filtering sensitive content, then deduplicating beyond the document level, since one document can still repeat a sensitive fragment many times. Helpfully, they are candid that sanitising data is a first line of defence rather than outright prevention.

What I find valuable in your framing is that it accommodates this honesty. The preventive measures do exist and are well documented, yet their own authors describe them as partial, which is a stronger version of your point than simply saying the industry has not tried. Given the Google episode you describe, do you see the main constraint as technical, or as the incentives facing the organisations best placed to act?

### Response to Dzemal

[View the post on the module forum](https://www.my-course.co.uk/mod/forum/discuss.php?d=390553#p797154)

*In reply to Dzemal's post on harmful and biased output from AI writers.*

Your risk-tiered framework is the most practical proposal I have read in this thread, and the Abid, Farooqi and Zou finding on anti-Muslim completions gives it real evidential weight. I want to pick up the human verification measure you attach to the high-risk tier, because the bias literature suggests some useful ways to reinforce it.

Weidinger et al. (2021) frame the underlying difficulty as knowing what to look for. Because prejudices shift with the decade and differ from one place to the next, they argue no single team can stay current on the full set that might wound or offend, and that the people best placed to recognise a given one are usually those who have been on the receiving end of it. This seems to me to strengthen rather than undercut your tier, since it identifies what the reviewer needs in order to do the job you are asking of them.

Their remedies accordingly sit alongside review rather than replacing it. They point to participatory mechanisms that operate over time rather than as one-off consultations, and to broad and transparent dataset documentation so that whose biases are encoded becomes inspectable. They also observe that fairness cannot be settled once at training time, because affected communities cannot be identified before a concrete application exists, which fits your argument that the appropriate level of autonomy depends on the deployment context.

One addition worth considering is that the tier might govern more than the intensity of review. Weidinger et al. (2021) cite a recruitment classifier abandoned because no unbiased training data could be constructed, which suggests declining to deploy is itself a legitimate outcome of a high-risk assessment. Would you fold that option into your framework, or keep it separate?

### Response to Jeroen

[View the post on the module forum](https://www.my-course.co.uk/mod/forum/discuss.php?d=390523#p797161)

*In reply to Jeroen's post on AI writers in administrative work.*

Your framing of the question as how much responsibility we hand over, rather than whether the tools are simply good or bad, is the most useful reframing in this thread. The preventive measure you attach to it, that someone still needs to check the output, is clearly right, and Shao (2025) offers some detail on what would make that check hold up.

Shao (2025) notes that remedies such as fact-checking and accuracy nudges were built on the premise that somebody meant what they wrote, which leaves them poorly matched to text produced without any belief, any wish to persuade, or any attempt to mislead. A model has no epistemic awareness that would let it recognise a fabrication. Shao adds that platform guardrails and retrieval-augmented generation improve factual accuracy without eliminating the problem, because the underlying mechanism remains next-token prediction. That supports your conclusion that the human stays responsible, since the technical layer cannot carry the responsibility on its own.

The demand side is where your point about detection becoming harder gains extra force. Shao (2025) observes that the confident register in which a fabrication arrives tends to earn it either belief or a merely cursory read, so the same fluency that makes the draft useful also works against the reader checking it.

That suggests some ways to give your reviewer better odds. Requiring the tool to surface its sources lets a claim be traced rather than judged on how it sounds, and assigning review to whoever answers for the claim rather than for the prose keeps the incentive in the right place. Both seem to me like extensions of the responsibility question you raise. Would you locate the accountability with the drafter or the reviewer?

## Summary Post (Unit 10)

[View the post on the module forum](https://www.my-course.co.uk/mod/forum/discuss.php?d=382436#p799193)

My initial post argued that the productivity gains Noy and Zhang (2023) measured came from substitution rather than collaboration, since most participants submitted the output barely touched. The responses have persuaded me that this framing was too blunt.

Damiano reframed the divide as augmentation against delegation rather than humans against machines, pointing to evidence that the same tool strengthens or weakens reasoning depending on whether the user interrogates what it returns. That distinction does more work than mine, because it explains why identical technology produces opposite results rather than treating the outcome as fixed.

Julio and Lana then showed why leaving that choice to the user is unreliable. Julio described a subgroup whose prompting volume looked healthy while almost none of it involved active cognitive direction, so engagement cannot be read off usage. Lana made the structural case, arguing that safeguards belong in the design of the system and the governance around it rather than in the self-regulation of each reader.

Her point lands against my own argument. I claimed fluent prose defeats ordinary scepticism, which is precisely the reason a reader is the wrong last line of defence. Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos (2021) show the alternative works, since cognitive forcing designs significantly reduced overreliance compared with simple explainable approaches, though participants gave the least favourable ratings to whichever design reduced it most. Prevention is therefore available but unpopular, which seems the more useful problem.

My revised position is that Bender et al. (2021) are right that coherence belongs to the reader, and that this is an argument for moving responsibility upstream. Measures such as documented intended and out-of-scope uses (Mitchell et al., 2019) place the judgement where it can be made deliberately, before anyone is reading under the influence of fluency.
