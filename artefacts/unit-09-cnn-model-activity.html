Convolutional Neural Networks: Object Recognition and its Implications¶

Unit 9 e-Portfolio activity for Machine Learning (PCOM7E). The brief has two parts. The first is to read Wall (2019) and record thoughts on the ethical and social implications of this technology. The second is to run the supplied CIFAR-10 object recognition model, review the sections of the algorithm, and change the index of the test image used for prediction to see whether the model classifies it correctly.

Both parts are treated here as one piece of work rather than two, because the measured behaviour of the model turns out to be the most direct evidence for the ethical argument. Where the classifier fails, and how confident it is when it fails, is precisely what Wall (2019) reports going wrong in deployment.

Dataset: CIFAR-10, 60,000 colour images of 32x32 pixels across ten object classes, collected by Krizhevsky, Nair and Hinton.

Preprocessing¶

Two transformations are applied. Pixel values are divided by 255 so that every input lies between 0 and 1, which keeps the gradients on a comparable scale across channels. The labels are converted to a one-hot encoding, so that class 3 becomes a vector with a single 1 in the fourth position rather than the number 3, which prevents the network from inferring an ordering among classes that does not exist.

Holding the test set back until the very end is a methodological point rather than a formality. The validation set guides decisions during training, so it stops being an unbiased estimate of performance the moment it influences a choice. Only the test set, which nothing has been tuned against, gives a realistic impression of how the model would behave on data it has never encountered.

Building the model¶

The architecture follows the supplied notebook: two convolution and pooling pairs, then a flatten, a dense hidden layer of 256 units, and a ten-way softmax output.

Each convolutional layer slides a set of learned filters across the image, so the same feature is detected wherever it appears rather than being tied to a fixed position. The pooling layers halve the spatial dimensions, which reduces computation and makes the representation tolerant of small shifts. The flatten converts the remaining feature maps into a single vector for the dense layers to classify.

In [5]:
recognitionModel = Sequential([
    Input(shape=(32, 32, 3)),
    Conv2D(filters=32, kernel_size=(4, 4), activation="relu"),
    MaxPool2D(pool_size=(2, 2)),
    Conv2D(filters=32, kernel_size=(4, 4), activation="relu"),
    MaxPool2D(pool_size=(2, 2)),
    Flatten(),
    Dense(256, activation="relu"),
    Dense(10, activation="softmax"),
])

recognitionModel.compile(
    loss="categorical_crossentropy", optimizer="adam", metrics=["accuracy"]
)

recognitionModel.summary()

Training¶

Early stopping watches the validation loss and calls a halt once two epochs pass without an improvement. The purpose is to catch the moment at which further training stops buying generalisation and begins merely memorising the particular images in the training split.

trainingHistory = recognitionModel.fit(
    trainImages, trainLabels,
    epochs=25,
    validation_data=(validationImages, validationLabels),
    callbacks=[stopEarly],
    verbose=0,
)

Accuracy is not distributed evenly¶

The overall figure conceals a wide spread between classes. Because the test set holds exactly 1,000 images of each class, this spread cannot be explained by imbalanced representation. Some categories are simply harder for this model than others.

The errors are structured rather than random. The confusions concentrate among the animal classes, with dog against cat the largest single pairing in the table above. At 32 by 32 pixels a cat and a dog share a great deal: four legs, a tail, fur, and a similar posture. The vehicle classes separate more cleanly because their outlines are rigid and distinctive, and where they do collide it is between the pairs that genuinely resemble one another: automobile against truck, and ship against airplane, both of which share a silhouette against an open background.

This is the first point that carries directly into the ethical discussion. The model's mistakes are not spread evenly across the population it is asked to classify; they cluster on the categories that resemble one another most closely.

The ethical and social implications¶

Wall (2019) reports on facial recognition rather than object recognition, but the mechanism that produces the failures he describes is the one measured above.

His central point is that the errors land unequally. He draws on work by Joy Buolamwini at the MIT Media Lab and by Timnit Gebru, who at the time led Google's ethics team for the field on the technical side, finding that these systems grow worse at separating one sex from another as the subject's complexion darkens, to the extent that darker-skinned women are disproportionately read as male. Gebru traces the cause to who is present in the material the systems learn from, which skews pale and male, and reports failure rates that differ starkly depending on complexion and sex.

The per-class results above illustrate the same phenomenon, and in one respect more sharply. CIFAR-10 supplies precisely a thousand test images for every category, and the recall still ranges widely between the strongest and the weakest class. Equal representation eliminated one possible cause of uneven performance and the unevenness remained, because certain categories are simply harder to tell apart than others. Anything deployed in the field inherits both difficulties simultaneously: the skewed material Gebru identifies, and the intrinsic separability problem demonstrated here.

What follows from an error depends entirely on what the classification is used for. Wall (2019) cites analysis from the University of Essex finding that four identifications in five during the Metropolitan Police pilots were mistaken, a rate he suggests could put people wrongly in the frame and erode their privacy. He lists trials by forces across South Wales, London, Manchester and Leicester, which alarmed campaign groups including Liberty and Big Brother Watch, whose objection is that each false hit places someone blameless under suspicion. A misfiled image in this notebook costs nothing at all. The identical failure rate turned on a pedestrian produces a police stop.

The confidence figures make the accountability question concrete. This model is regularly wrong while reporting a high score, and those scores overlap with the ones attached to its correct answers, so somebody shown only a match and a number cannot distinguish a sound identification from a spurious one. Wall (2019) notes that Amazon has gone on supplying its Rekognition product to American forces while maintaining that what buyers do with it is not the company's concern. That stance is hard to defend when the product withholds from its buyer any means of telling when it should not be believed.

Consent is the remaining question, and it does not arise in this notebook at all. Everything used here was gathered for research, and no object in a photograph suffers from being mislabelled. The situation Wall (2019) sets out is the reverse. Gebru puts the number of American adults whose faces are already enrolled in such systems at roughly 130 million. Wall describes a shopper in Britain who went to court over South Wales Police capturing his image while he went about his day, and records the then Information Commissioner warning that no proper statutory basis governed any of it. He also notes San Francisco prohibiting municipal and police deployment outright, on the grounds that the technology is both unreliable and corrosive of liberty.

My own view is that the technical and the ethical questions are less separable than they are usually treated. The measurements in this notebook are ordinary machine learning practice: a confusion matrix, per-class recall, a check on confidence. Yet each one maps onto a specific harm Wall (2019) documents. The confusion matrix shows which groups absorb the errors, the per-class recall shows that balanced data does not guarantee balanced outcomes, and the confidence comparison shows that the system cannot flag its own failures. A developer who reports only the headline accuracy has not merely given an incomplete technical account; they have withheld precisely the information a reader would need to judge whether the system is fit to deploy. That is why the reporting standard is itself an ethical question rather than a matter of technical thoroughness.
